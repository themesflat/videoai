The first formula is the monochrome column: a top and a pair of wide-leg pants in the exact same tone, floor to collarbone, worn with a heel or a pointed flat in a color that keeps the line going rather than stopping it. When there's no visible break between where the top ends and the pants begin, the eye stops measuring your body in two separate pieces, waist-up and waist-down, and instead reads you as one continuous vertical shape, which is exactly what makes a person look taller from across a room.

This works because contrast is what creates a stopping point for the eye, and a stopping point is what makes a body read as shorter, cut into sections rather than one sweeping line. For the office, a camel wide-leg pant with a camel silk blouse and nude pointed flats disappears into one elegant column under fluorescent light. Running errands, a soft charcoal knit top over charcoal wide-leg pants with a charcoal ballet flat feels easy and put-together without trying hard. For evening, the same principle in black, a black silk cami, black wide-leg trousers, and a black heel, with one piece of gold jewelry as the only interruption in the whole line.

Picture stepping out of an elevator in that single unbroken column of camel, catching your reflection in the glass doors, and realizing you're standing taller in the very same trousers that made you feel two inches shorter last month in a busy print top.
